# PredictFun: LMSR Price Discovery And Band-Pass Graduation Clearing

Matthew Brown

June 2026

WORKING DRAFT, rev. 0.3

## Abstract

This paper studies the liquidity bootstrapping problem for binary fixed-payout prediction markets. Thin liquidity makes early price discovery noisy; subsidy can smooth prices but scales poorly across many markets. The mechanism uses a Logarithmic Market Scoring Rule (LMSR) before graduation, but not as a funded automated market maker. Pre-graduation LMSR is a demand-pricing surface: traders move a continuous probability curve and receive provisional receipts that record the exact path segment they bought. No pre-graduation receipt is a final redeemable outcome token.

Graduation is the conversion step from priced demand to fixed-payout exposure. The protocol clears only the LMSR price bands traversed by both YES and NO demand in opposite directions. This band-pass clearing rule passes compatible path segments, refunds incompatible path segments, and mints final YES/NO claims only where collateral completeness can be proven locally. Aggregate math, average receipt prices, virtual reserves, or global overbet proration are insufficient because they can preserve headline balances while losing the path-level cost basis needed for fixed-payout solvency.

The result is a prediction-market launch mechanism with immediate probability discovery, no protocol market-maker loss budget, and deterministic graduation into fully collateralized fixed-payout exposure. Its central tradeoff is explicit: early participation is provisional until enough path-compatible opposing demand exists.

## 1. Problem Statement: Liquidity Bootstrapping

Prediction markets have a liquidity bootstrapping problem. A new market needs a price before it has deep two-sided liquidity, but thin liquidity produces high slippage. When a small order can move the market dramatically, the displayed price becomes a noisy function of order size rather than a clean estimate of belief.

The direct solution is subsidy: seed each market with enough capital that early trades do not move price too much. That works for a small number of important markets, but it scales poorly when the goal is to launch many markets. The protocol either needs a large treasury, accepts shallow prices, or spends scarce capital on markets that may never attract organic demand. It may also simply not have the money.

A central limit order book solves a different part of the problem. It gives precise matching once makers and takers arrive, but it needs offchain infrastructure and it does not necessarily give immediate price discovery. An empty or thin book is not a smooth probability curve; it is a waiting room for limit orders.

LMSR comes close to the desired launch primitive. It can quote immediately, move smoothly under sparse flow, and express probabilities directly rather than through reserve ratios. The missing piece is subsidy. In conventional LMSR, the liquidity parameter `b` is tied to a funded market-maker loss budget. This paper asks whether prediction markets can keep LMSR's price-discovery surface while removing the requirement that every new market be subsidized.

The proposed answer is to make pre-graduation LMSR virtual. The `b` parameter controls price smoothness, not funded inventory, and pre-graduation bets are treated as priced intents rather than final outcome-token purchases. Graduation is the separate step that decides which intents can become fixed-payout claims.

```text
Pre-graduation: LMSR-priced intents.
Graduation: band-pass clearing over compatible LMSR path segments.
Post-graduation exposure: fixed-payout YES/NO claims backed by matched collateral.
```

This gives launch markets immediate price discovery without pretending that every displayed quote is already a fully backed fill.

## 2. Background: Existing Bootstrapping Choices

### Fixed-Payout Prediction Claims

A binary prediction market outcome token has a fixed terminal payoff:

```math
\begin{aligned}
1\,\mathrm{YES} &\mapsto 1\,\text{unit of collateral} && \text{if YES resolves true} \\
1\,\mathrm{NO} &\mapsto 1\,\text{unit of collateral} && \text{if NO resolves true}
\end{aligned}
```

For a collateral-complete binary market:

```math
1\,\mathrm{YES}+1\,\mathrm{NO}=1\,\text{unit of collateral}
```

This constraint makes prediction-market launch different from token launch. A token bonding curve can sell an asset whose final redemption value is not fixed. A prediction market cannot safely sell 100 final YES claims at a price of 0.05, collect 5 units of collateral, and later owe 100 units if YES wins unless some other source funds the missing 95 units.

Fully collateralized token frameworks are the right destination once liquidity exists. Polymarket's [Conditional Token Framework (CTF)](https://docs.polymarket.com/trading/ctf/overview), built on Gnosis Conditional Tokens, is a good example: YES and NO are conventional outcome tokens backed by complete sets of collateral. The bootstrapping problem is not that this destination is bad. It is that a new market still has to discover price and attract opposing demand before there are enough complete sets worth trading.

### Pari-Mutuel Pools

Pari-mutuel markets avoid this insolvency by changing the payoff. Bettors on the winning side share the pool after betting closes. This is solvent without subsidy, but it does not give the user a fixed-payout YES or NO token at the quoted price. Final odds depend on the final pool composition, so the launch mechanism gives weaker price certainty than the fixed-payout product users expect from tokenized prediction markets.

### Constant-Product And Fixed-Product Market Makers

Constant-product and fixed-product market makers quote from reserves. In a binary fixed-product market, an approximate YES price is:

```math
P_{\mathrm{yes}}=\frac{B_{\mathrm{no}}}{B_{\mathrm{yes}}+B_{\mathrm{no}}}
```

A 5% YES price therefore implies:

```math
B_{\mathrm{yes}}\approx 19B_{\mathrm{no}}
```

If those reserves are real, the market starts with highly imbalanced inventory and liquidity providers take outcome exposure before demand is proven. If the reserves are virtual, the displayed quote depth is not collateral that can pay winners. Reserve curves are useful once a market has committed liquidity; they do not by themselves solve no-subsidy launch.

### Logarithmic Market Scoring Rule

For a binary market, LMSR uses the cost function:

```math
C(q_{\mathrm{yes}},q_{\mathrm{no}})
=b\ln\left(e^{q_{\mathrm{yes}}/b}+e^{q_{\mathrm{no}}/b}\right)
```

The marginal YES price is:

```math
P_{\mathrm{yes}}
=\frac{e^{q_{\mathrm{yes}}/b}}{e^{q_{\mathrm{yes}}/b}+e^{q_{\mathrm{no}}/b}}
```

and:

```math
P_{\mathrm{no}}=1-P_{\mathrm{yes}}
```

The cost of buying `s` YES shares is:

```math
\mathrm{cost}_{\mathrm{yes}}
=C(q_{\mathrm{yes}}+s,q_{\mathrm{no}})-C(q_{\mathrm{yes}},q_{\mathrm{no}})
```

LMSR is attractive for sparse launch markets because its state is a probability score rather than reserve inventory. It can start from a meaningful prior, quote continuously, and move smoothly as demand arrives.

The cost is that conventional LMSR is not free. The parameter `b` implies a bounded market-maker loss:

```math
\mathrm{max\ loss}=b\ln 2
```

That bound is a funding requirement. If nobody funds it, LMSR cannot be treated as a seller of final fixed-payout claims.

## 3. The Virtual `b` Problem

This design keeps LMSR as the pre-graduation price-discovery surface but changes what its state means. Before graduation:

```text
q_yes and q_no are demand-pricing state.
b is virtual smoothness, not funded inventory.
pre-graduation bets are priced intents.
receipt collateral remains escrowed.
final fixed-payout claims are created only after clearing.
```

The displayed LMSR price is therefore an implied probability and a pricing path, not a fill guarantee. This is what makes no-subsidy launch possible, but it also creates the central technical problem of the paper: virtual `b` can quote a price without proving that the quoted path is backed by compatible opposite-side demand.

If the protocol simply treated virtual LMSR buys as final tokens, it would recreate the missing-subsidy problem. If it globally prorated receipts by aggregate shares or aggregate collateral, it could retain exposure from price regions that were never funded by the other side. The solution must preserve the LMSR path each intent traversed and clear only the bands where YES and NO demand actually overlap.

This is why pre-graduation trades become receipts. A receipt is not a settled YES or NO token. It is a priced intent plus a path record. Graduation clearing later asks:

```text
Did real YES and NO demand traverse the same LMSR probability bands?
```

Only the path-compatible answer becomes fixed-payout exposure.

## 4. Starting Away From Even Odds

Prediction markets often should not begin at 50/50. A market may reasonably open with YES at 5%, 20%, 80%, or another prior. LMSR handles this cleanly with an initial state offset.

Let:

```math
\begin{aligned}
r &= q_{\mathrm{yes}}-q_{\mathrm{no}} \\
P_{\mathrm{yes}}(r) &= \frac{1}{1+e^{-r/b}}
\end{aligned}
```

To start at probability `P0`:

```math
r_0=b\ln\left(\frac{P_0}{1-P_0}\right)
```

For a 5% YES starting probability:

```math
\begin{aligned}
r_0 &= b\ln\left(\frac{0.05}{0.95}\right) \\
r_0 &\approx -2.9444b
\end{aligned}
```

This offset is not inventory and not collateral. It is only the coordinate from which demand-pricing begins. The market can quote a 5% YES probability without pretending that the protocol already owns a deep reserve of final NO inventory or a funded pool of cheap YES claims.

## 5. Receipts As Path Records

Each pre-graduation buy creates a receipt lot:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{owner}_\ell &: \text{receipt owner} \\
\mathrm{side}_\ell &\in \{\mathrm{YES},\mathrm{NO}\} \\
s_\ell &: \text{provisional share width} \\
c_\ell &: \text{LMSR path cost basis} \\
(q_{\mathrm{yes},\ell}^{0},q_{\mathrm{no},\ell}^{0}) &: \text{state before the intent} \\
(q_{\mathrm{yes},\ell}^{1},q_{\mathrm{no},\ell}^{1}) &: \text{state after the intent} \\
\tau_\ell &: \text{sequence index}
\end{aligned}
```

The key path coordinate is:

```math
r=q_{\mathrm{yes}}-q_{\mathrm{no}}
```

A YES buy increases `r`; a NO buy decreases `r`. Each receipt therefore traverses an interval:

```math
\begin{aligned}
r_{\mathrm{low},l} &= \min(r_{\mathrm{before},l},r_{\mathrm{after},l}) \\
r_{\mathrm{high},l} &= \max(r_{\mathrm{before},l},r_{\mathrm{after},l}) \\
\mathrm{width}_l &= r_{\mathrm{high},l}-r_{\mathrm{low},l}=\mathrm{shares}_l
\end{aligned}
```

The receipt's cost basis is the integral of the relevant marginal LMSR price over that interval:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{YES\_cost}[u,v] &= \int_u^v P_{\mathrm{yes}}(r)\,dr \\
\mathrm{NO\_cost}[u,v] &= \int_u^v P_{\mathrm{no}}(r)\,dr
\end{aligned}
```

A receipt's average price:

```math
\mathrm{average\_price}_l=\frac{\mathrm{cost\_basis}_l}{\mathrm{shares}_l}
```

is useful for display, but it is not enough for partial clearing. The cost basis is distributed unevenly along the path because marginal prices change as the trade moves the curve.

## 6. Why Aggregate Math Is Not Enough

Several tempting shortcuts look solvent at the aggregate level but fail at the level required for fixed-payout claims.

### Bounded LMSR Loss Without Funding

The classical LMSR loss bound is a funding requirement, not a free solvency proof. If the protocol sells final outcome tokens from an LMSR and does not fund `b * ln(2)`, it has simply hidden a subsidy assumption. A no-subsidy protocol must not rely on that bound to pay winners.

### Global Share Proration

Suppose total YES shares and total NO shares are equal. That does not imply the two sides traversed the same price path. A YES wave from 5% to 20% and a later NO wave from 80% to 65% may have equal share widths, but they do not overlap in LMSR coordinate space. Retaining both sides because aggregate quantities balance would create fixed-payout claims from incompatible demand.

### Collateral Proration

Matching total YES collateral to total NO collateral also fails. A small number of high-price shares can contribute the same collateral as many low-price shares, while creating very different payout liabilities. Fixed-payout solvency depends on retained share count, not only retained dollars.

### Average-Price Matching

A receipt that moves YES from 50% to 60% pays lower marginal prices near 50% and higher marginal prices near 60%. If only the 55% to 60% segment is matched, the retained cost is the cost of that upper segment, not the receipt's average price across the whole 50% to 60% path.

Using average price for a partial fill can over-refund one segment, under-refund another, or lock the wrong amount of collateral. The clearing rule must preserve path cost, not just receipt-level averages.

### Virtual-Reserve Accounting

Virtual reserves can produce attractive quotes, but virtual liquidity cannot redeem winning fixed-payout claims. If a pre-graduation CPMM uses virtual reserves to imitate depth, the mechanism still needs a later rule deciding which claims are backed by real collateral. Band-pass clearing is that rule; without it, the virtual-reserve math only postpones the solvency question.

## 7. Band-Pass Clearing

Band-pass clearing treats the LMSR path as the object to be matched. It passes only probability bands crossed by both sides in opposite directions.

The frozen receipt book is converted into intervals on `r`. The algorithm:

1. Collects every receipt interval endpoint.
2. Sorts and deduplicates endpoints.
3. Sweeps each adjacent band `[r_k, r_{k+1}]`.
4. Finds YES receipts covering the whole band.
5. Finds NO receipts covering the whole band.
6. Retains matched exposure only if both active sets are non-empty.
7. Retains the scarce side fully within the band.
8. Retains the crowded side pro-rata within the band.
9. Refunds every unretained path segment at its actual LMSR path cost.

For band width:

```math
w_k=r_{k+1}-r_k
```

and active counts:

```math
\begin{aligned}
Y_k &= \#\{\text{YES receipts covering the band}\} \\
N_k &= \#\{\text{NO receipts covering the band}\} \\
m_k &= \min(Y_k,N_k)
\end{aligned}
```

the retained fractions are:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{YES\_fraction}_k &= \frac{m_k}{Y_k} \\
\mathrm{NO\_fraction}_k &= \frac{m_k}{N_k}
\end{aligned}
```

The matched fixed-payout market cap contributed by the band is:

```math
F_k=w_k\,m_k
```

This is "band-pass" in the literal sense: a band of the price path passes graduation only if opposite-side demand also crossed that band. One-sided drift, non-overlapping reversals, and aggregate-only balance are filtered out.

## 8. Local Collateral Completeness

The solvency property comes from the complementarity of binary LMSR prices:

```math
P_{\mathrm{yes}}(r)+P_{\mathrm{no}}(r)=1
```

For any band `[u, v]`:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{YES\_cost}[u,v]+\mathrm{NO\_cost}[u,v]
&=\int_u^v\left(P_{\mathrm{yes}}(r)+P_{\mathrm{no}}(r)\right)\,dr \\
&=\int_u^v 1\,dr \\
&=v-u
\end{aligned}
```

The width `v - u` is also the number of fixed-payout YES/NO complete sets represented by that band. Therefore, when a YES path segment and a NO path segment overlap over the same band, their combined cost basis exactly funds the complete set for that band.

For each cleared band:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{locked\_collateral}_k
&=m_k(\mathrm{YES\_cost}_k+\mathrm{NO\_cost}_k) \\
&=m_k\,w_k \\
&=F_k
\end{aligned}
```

Summing over all retained bands:

```math
\mathrm{locked\_collateral}=\sum_k F_k=F
```

where `F` is the retained fixed-payout market cap. If all unretained path cost is refunded:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{total\_escrow} &= \mathrm{refunds}+\mathrm{locked\_collateral} \\
\mathrm{total\_escrow} &= \mathrm{refunds}+F
\end{aligned}
```

Thus the clearing rule mints final claims only where the locked collateral equals the maximum payout obligation.

## 9. Graduation Threshold

Graduation is the point of the design, not a cosmetic milestone. Virtual LMSR is a bootstrapping surface: it gives a new market continuous odds before the market has conventional liquidity. Once enough path-compatible demand exists, the mechanism should leave the virtual phase and move into ordinary fixed-payout market structure as soon as possible.

The reason is practical. A CTF-style market is better once it has liquidity: claims are ordinary collateralized YES/NO tokens, liquidity can be provided or quoted by conventional market makers, and traders no longer need to reason about provisional intents. Keeping a market on virtual LMSR after it can support fixed-payout exposure would preserve bootstrapping complexity after the bootstrap has done its job.

Graduation should be based on path-compatible filled market cap, not provisional volume, headline open interest, total escrow, or final LMSR price.

Define:

```math
F=\sum_{\text{cleared bands }k}F_k
```

The market graduates only if:

```math
F\ge \mathrm{graduation\_threshold}
```

If the threshold is not met, the no-subsidy rule is simple:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{retained\_shares}_l &= 0 \\
\mathrm{refund}_l &= \mathrm{cost\_basis}_l
\end{aligned}
```

for every active receipt. Sub-threshold overlap may be mathematically solvent, but the threshold defines the minimum viable fixed-payout market. This keeps graduation from producing tiny, fragile markets whose apparent precision exceeds their real liquidity.

The threshold can be paired with non-price research gates, such as concentration limits or minimum participant counts, but those gates are external to the clearing math. The clearing primitive remains `F`: the amount of fixed-payout exposure that survived band-pass matching.

## 10. Worked Examples

The examples below use contiguous price histories from a single LMSR state. A pair of disjoint intervals such as "YES moves 5% to 20%" and "NO moves 80% to 65%" is not a literal market history by itself; the price must have moved through the intervening states first. Non-overlap appears as unretraced parts of one continuous path.

### Example A: 5% Starting Probability

Let a market start at 5% YES:

```math
r_0=b\ln\left(\frac{0.05}{0.95}\right)
```

Early YES demand moves the market from 5% to 8%. Later NO demand moves it from 8% back to 6%.

The YES path covers:


```math
8\%\to 6\%
```

```math

```

and refunds the unmatched YES segment:

```math
5\%\to 6\%
```

Let:

```math
r_p=b\ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)
```

The retained fixed-payout exposure is the matched LMSR-coordinate width:

```math
\begin{aligned}
F_A &= r_{0.08}-r_{0.06} \\
\mathrm{locked}_A
&=\int_{r_{0.06}}^{r_{0.08}}\left(P_{\mathrm{yes}}(r)+P_{\mathrm{no}}(r)\right)\,dr \\
&=F_A
\end{aligned}
```

The refund is the unmatched part of the YES path at its actual LMSR path cost:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{refund}_{\mathrm{YES}}
&=\int_{r_{0.05}}^{r_{0.06}}P_{\mathrm{yes}}(r)\,dr \\
&=b\ln\left(\frac{1+e^{r_{0.06}/b}}{1+e^{r_{0.05}/b}}\right) \\
\mathrm{refund}_{\mathrm{NO}}&=0
\end{aligned}
```

This is the behavior desired for low-probability markets. LMSR can start at 5% and produce smooth early odds, but final fixed-payout exposure appears only where real opposite-side demand retraced the same probability band.

```math

```

At minimum, that part of the large YES receipt is refunded at path cost:

```math
\mathrm{refund}_{\mathrm{large\ YES}}
\ge \int_{r_{0.05}}^{r_{0.32}}P_{\mathrm{yes}}(r)\,dr
```

The small repeated overlap near 32% to 35% can still graduate if it meets the threshold. This is almost a feature: the mechanism keeps the discovered price movement, while refusing to pretend that the entire drift has already become funded fixed-payout exposure.

```price-chart
  "caption": "Because both Alice and Bea cover the 55% to 60% band, the current rule prorates the YES side instead of giving Alice FIFO priority."

```

On the 55% to 60% band:

```math
\begin{aligned}
Y_{55\%\to60\%} &= 2 && \text{Alice and Bea} \\
N_{55\%\to60\%} &= 1 && \text{Noah} \\
m_{55\%\to60\%} &= 1 \\
\mathrm{YES\_fraction}_{55\%\to60\%} &= \frac{1}{2} \\
\mathrm{NO\_fraction}_{55\%\to60\%} &= 1
\end{aligned}
```

Noah's NO path is fully matched. Alice and Bea each retain half of the 55% to 60% YES band. Alice's 50% to 55% segment is refunded, Bea's 60% to 65% segment is refunded, and each receives a refund for the half of the shared 55% to 60% band that did not pass.

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Alice}_{\mathrm{refund}}
&=\int_{r_{0.50}}^{r_{0.55}}P_{\mathrm{yes}}(r)\,dr
  +\frac{1}{2}\int_{r_{0.55}}^{r_{0.60}}P_{\mathrm{yes}}(r)\,dr \\
\mathrm{Bea}_{\mathrm{refund}}
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{r_{0.55}}^{r_{0.60}}P_{\mathrm{yes}}(r)\,dr
  +\int_{r_{0.60}}^{r_{0.65}}P_{\mathrm{yes}}(r)\,dr \\
\mathrm{Noah}_{\mathrm{refund}}&=0
\end{aligned}
```

A FIFO variant could instead match Noah entirely against Alice's earlier YES receipt and refund Bea's 55% to 60% segment. That is a different allocation policy. The mechanism analyzed here uses pro-rata matching within each band because solvency depends on path overlap, while participant priority is a policy layer that must be specified explicitly.

## 11. Research Properties

### No Protocol Subsidy

The mechanism never relies on an unfunded LMSR loss budget. It refunds unbacked path segments instead of socializing their missing collateral.

### Continuous Price Discovery

Every market has a live LMSR probability curve before final liquidity exists. The curve communicates demand and belief without promising settlement exposure.

### Fixed-Payout Discipline

The mechanism does not become pari-mutuel. Retained claims are fixed-payout YES or NO exposure, and the clearing rule locks one unit of collateral per complete set.

### Cost-Basis Preservation

Each receipt is split by path segment. Retained cost plus refund equals the original receipt cost basis:

```math
\mathrm{retained\_cost}_l+\mathrm{refund}_l=\mathrm{cost\_basis}_l
```

### Deterministic Clearing

Given the same frozen receipt book, `b`, initial state, and rounding rules, every implementation should produce the same bands, retained shares, locked collateral, and refunds.

## 12. Open Research Questions

Several parameters remain empirical rather than purely mathematical:

1. How should `b` vary by market category, expected volatility, and initial probability?
2. What graduation threshold produces enough post-graduation liquidity without refunding too many useful small markets?
3. How should the interface communicate provisional receipts, expected retention, and band-specific refund risk?
4. What rounding policy best preserves deterministic clearing under onchain integer arithmetic?
5. Which anti-manipulation gates should be applied before allowing a minimally matched book to graduate?

These questions do not change the solvency argument. They determine whether the mechanism feels liquid, legible, and resistant to strategic path shaping.

## 13. Conclusion

LMSR is a strong pre-graduation mechanism for prediction markets because it gives smooth probability discovery without requiring reserve inventory at every prior. It is especially useful for markets that start far from 50%, such as 5% YES events, where CPMM-style reserve ratios either create shallow real liquidity or rely on virtual depth.

But LMSR math alone does not make no-subsidy fixed-payout claims solvent. Classical LMSR needs a funded loss budget, aggregate matching ignores path compatibility, and average prices lose the marginal cost structure needed for partial fills.

Band-pass graduation clearing supplies the missing step. It matches only opposing demand that traversed the same LMSR probability bands, locks exactly the collateral needed for those retained fixed-payout claims, and refunds the rest. The mechanism therefore separates three ideas that are often conflated at launch: probability discovery, fill, and settlement backing.
